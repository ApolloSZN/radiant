# America's hidden transformer-steel dependence

*Every number below is generated from Radiant's code (`scripts/build_goes_article.py`). Ranges are bounds, not confidence intervals. Method: [methods note](methods.md).*

Every transformer on the American grid, from the gray can on a utility pole to a 300-ton unit at a substation, is built around a core of one specialized material: grain-oriented electrical steel, or GOES. It is rolled and annealed so its crystals line up and magnetism flows with little loss. The United States has exactly one company that makes it, Cleveland-Cliffs, at Butler, Pennsylvania and Zanesville, Ohio.

The usual story is reassuring. Official trade data show the U.S. imported only about 27.9 thousand tonnes (kt) of GOES sheet in 2019 and exported 45.7 kt, which makes America look like a net exporter. A 2021 Commerce Department report put U.S. use at "approximately 220,000 metric tons per year," so sheet imports looked like a small slice. Radiant's own earlier headline said about a third of U.S. use was foreign.

That picture is wrong. It counts the steel that arrives as rolls of sheet and misses the steel that arrives already shaped into transformer cores, or already inside finished transformers. Counting all three, the U.S. used **282–376 kt of GOES in 2019, and 51–65% of it was foreign.**

## Three doors, one counted

GOES enters American transformers three ways.

**As sheet.** This is what most trade statistics track. Sheet imports come mostly from Japan and Korea, and were 27.9 kt in 2019 ([UN Comtrade, U.S.-reported data](https://comtradeapi.un.org/public/v1/preview/C/A/HS?reporterCode=842&period=2019&cmdCode=722511,722611&flowCode=M)). But 10.9 kt of U.S. "exports" that year were foreign steel passing back out (re-exports), so only about 17 kt of foreign sheet actually stayed.

**Inside cores.** Many U.S. transformer makers no longer cut and stack their own cores. They buy finished cores and laminations from plants in Canada and Mexico. Customs counts these in pieces, not kilograms, so the steel inside them is invisible in tonnage statistics. Commerce estimated that 68 kt of GOES came in this way in 2019 ([FR 2021-24958](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2021-11-18/pdf/2021-24958.pdf)), a figure it took from the Core Coalition, a group of core importers. By 2025 Canadian and Mexican core-part imports had nearly tripled in value. Adjusted for the GOES price, that suggests roughly 109 kt.

**Inside finished transformers.** The U.S. imported 617 of the roughly 750 large power transformers it installed in 2019 ([Commerce](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2021-11-18/pdf/2021-24958.pdf)). These weigh 150–400 tons each ([DOE, 2024](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/EXEC-2022-001242%20-%20Large%20Power%20Transformer%20Resilience%20Report%20signed%20by%20Secretary%20Granholm%20on%207-10-24.pdf)), and 40–48% of that weight is GOES ([DOE 2022](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/Electric%20Grid%20Supply%20Chain%20Report%20-%20Final.pdf); [NREL 2026](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy26osti/97167.pdf)). That puts 37–118 kt of foreign GOES inside imported large transformers alone. Add imported distribution transformers, about 18% of units, and the third door carried 68–157 kt in 2019, as much as or more than the cores.

## The number everyone cited was already counting cores

Radiant's earlier headline took Commerce's 220 kt as sheet use and added the 68 kt of cores on top, for 288 kt. Commerce's own report says otherwise. It calls sheet imports "less than 20 percent" of consumption, and it calls total foreign penetration, sheet plus cores, "approximately 44 percent." Those two statements fit together only if sheet use was about 146–151 kt and total use 213–218 kt. In other words, the 220 kt already included the cores. Adding them again counted 72 kt twice.

The same arithmetic resolves an apparent puzzle. With the inflated figure, U.S. production would have had to be about 239 kt, more than Cliffs' stated ceiling of about 227 kt for *all* electrical steel. With the corrected figure, 2019 production comes out at 164–169 kt. A 2017 check using Commerce's 37% import share gives 162 kt. Neither Cliffs nor its predecessor AK Steel has ever published GOES-only output. AK told the U.S. International Trade Commission in 2014 that its GOES capacity was about 259 kt ([USITC Pub. 4491](https://www.usitc.gov/publications/701_731/pub4491_.pdf)). In 2019 the mill was running well below that.

## The round trip

Something changed after 2020. Before then, the biggest buyers of U.S.-made GOES were Belgium and India; only 15% went to Canada and Mexico in 2019. From 2021 to 2025, **79–90% went to Canada and Mexico**, where North America's big core makers are ([U.S.-reported trade](https://comtradeapi.un.org/)). Canada's largest, JFE Shoji Power Canada (formerly Cogent Power), is now owned by the trading arm of a Japanese steel mill.

So some of the GOES inside imported cores is American steel coming home. Commerce's 2021 report assumed this double counting was "likely minimal because Canada was not a major destination for U.S. GOES exports." That was true when it was written and stopped being true soon after. It means the "net exporter" label overstates self-sufficiency. Radiant's ranges allow for every exported ton to return. Even then, the foreign share in 2019 is at least 51%.

## What the prices say

The market behaved like a tight one. The average price of imported GOES rose from $1.94/kg in 2019 to $4.04/kg in 2023 ([U.S.-reported trade](https://comtradeapi.un.org/)). The Bureau of Labor Statistics price index for transformer manufacturing rose 76% between 2019 and 2025 ([BLS PCU335311335311](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/PCU335311335311)). Lead times for large transformers reached three to five years ([DOE, 2024](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/EXEC-2022-001242%20-%20Large%20Power%20Transformer%20Resilience%20Report%20signed%20by%20Secretary%20Granholm%20on%207-10-24.pdf)). U.S. transformer imports grew from $2.2 billion in 2019 to $9.3 billion in 2025, and imported distribution transformers from 215,000 to 394,000 units a year.

Policy has mostly aimed at the sheet. Section 232 tariffs have applied to GOES since 2018 and rose to 50% in June 2025. Cores and laminations were added only in August 2025 ([CRS R48933](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48933.html)), and a 2020 agreement already excludes Mexican cores and transformers from any Section 232 transformer action ([USTR](https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2020/november/ustr-statement-successful-conclusion-steel-negotiations-mexico)). There are no anti-dumping duties on GOES: the 1994 orders were revoked in 2006 ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2006-03-28/pdf/E6-4477.pdf)), and the 2014 cases failed ([USITC](https://www.usitc.gov/press_room/news_release/2014/er1023mm2.htm)).

## Looking forward

Demand is set to grow on two fronts. Utilities are replacing an aging fleet of distribution transformers; NREL projects the capacity needed by 2050 at 160–260% of 2021 levels ([NREL, 2024](https://nrel.gov/docs/fy25osti/92076.pdf)). Building out transmission would add 94–114 kt of GOES a year ([NREL, 2026](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy26osti/97167.pdf)). Radiant's scenarios put total U.S. GOES use at 410–756 kt by 2035.

Supply has one lever. Cliffs says an upgrade at Butler will raise GOES output there by about 25% by 2028, from a base it hasn't disclosed. Meanwhile 11 transformer makers have announced about $1.46 billion of new U.S. and North American plants since 2022. None of them says where its steel will come from, and new assembly lines do not make new steel.

In Radiant's outlook, with no change the foreign share of U.S. GOES use reaches **70–84% by 2035**. If Cliffs completes its upgrade *and* keeps every ton at home, it is still 49–72%. In no scenario through 2035 does it fall below **46%**.

## Why one mill matters more than the tonnage suggests

Volume is only part of the exposure. DOE's 2022 supply-chain review found that the domestic mill "cannot produce the grades required for LPT core manufacturing," and that only mills in Japan, South Korea and Germany could make GOES to the specifications set by current DOE standards ([DOE 2022](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/Electric%20Grid%20Supply%20Chain%20Report%20-%20Final.pdf)). Interviewed manufacturers estimated that domestic supply met about a fifth of their needs. So even the U.S. tonnage that exists does not cover the part of the grid where a failure is hardest to replace. A large power transformer that fails today can take three to five years to replace, and the steel for its replacement will very likely come from abroad.

The supply base is also thin in a way trade totals hide. Allegheny Technologies, the only other U.S. producer, left the business in 2016 ([ATI 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018963/000101896317000007/atify201610-k.htm)). In 2023 Cliffs opened a 70,000-ton line for non-oriented electrical steel, the grade used in electric-vehicle motors, at the same Zanesville plant that finishes its GOES ([AIST](https://www.aist.org/cleveland-cliffs-commissions-noes-expansion)). It has also won a contract to supply up to 53,000 short tons of GOES to a federal stockpile over five years ([Steel Market Update](https://www.steelmarketupdate.com/2026/07/06/cliffs-awarded-400m-goes-contract-from-department-of-war/)). Steel set aside for a stockpile is steel that does not reach a transformer plant that year.

## What would sharpen the picture

Three facts would narrow these ranges more than anything else. First, Cliffs could report GOES output separately from its stainless and electrical totals. Today that single number has to be inferred from Commerce percentages. Second, customs could record the weight of imported cores and laminations, not just their count; the steel in that channel is now an index built on a single 2019 estimate from an interested party. Third, the mix of imported power transformers by size: the 2019 range of 37–118 kt for GOES in imported large transformers comes almost entirely from not knowing how big those units were.

## What this does and doesn't show

It does not show a shortage. Foreign supply is large and some of it is growing: JFE and JSW are building GOES capacity in India ([JFE, 2024](https://www.jfe-steel.co.jp/en/release/2024/02/240213.html)), though thyssenkrupp curtailed its French plant in 2026 ([thyssenkrupp](https://www.thyssenkrupp-steel.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/thyssenkrupp-electrical-steel-extends-production-cuts-at-its-isbergues-site-in-france.html)). Depending on allies is not the same as scarcity. It also does not give a single precise share. U.S. GOES production is published for no year at all, so outside 2017 and 2019 the figures rest on a stated model range, and the core and transformer channels are estimates whose methods are shown.

What it does show is that the right baseline is very different from the one in circulation. Most of the steel in America's transformers is foreign, and most of that dependence enters through doors that trade statistics don't weigh. Policy that watches only the sheet is watching the smallest door.
